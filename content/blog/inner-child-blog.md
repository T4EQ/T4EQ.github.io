## International Literacy Day and kids

Yesterday was International Literacy Day, and seeing so many campaigns online was inspiring. The real credit goes to the local communities, NGOs, teachers, and volunteers who do the heavy lifting every single day. These awareness days are important reminders and literacy is something that deserves our attention today, tomorrow, and everyday of the year. So let’s continue this reminder for another day. 

In our last [post](/blog/first-challenge-blog/), we shared our early hurdles starting from scratch and securing our first community partner. While we will dive into the technical scoping of our LEAP project soon, time and again we will bring in the human side that shapes a lot of what we build or hope to build. 

Sharing a little anecdote on this post from a field visit earlier this year.

## First field visit to our community partner
Earlier this year, our team visited one of AID India’s focus villages in Pudukkottai, Tamil Nadu. Tucked far off the main highway, we drove into a world we barely knew existed. The local coordinator welcomed us with a massive smile and took us around. The contrast hit hard instantly. We saw people surviving on daily wage labor, heard stories of young girls dropping out of school due to family situations, and noticed severe resource shortages, like a water tank way too small for a village of a few hundred people. Yet, everyone greeted us with genuine warmth, offering whatever they had to share with us in abundance. It is a striking irony that somehow those with the least to give are often the most generous, always.

Soon, a crowd of kids surrounded us, following us to the local education center with wide-eyed curiosity. They showed off their talents, played small instruments, and climbed trees for us to fetch fruit to share. We had actually gone there to field-test one of our [LEAP](/projects/) nodes, but the tech setup got lost in translation. We prepped our build for Wi-Fi, only to find a standalone LAN setup instead. It was a quick, humbling lesson in why local context matters, and a practical reminder of what we mentioned in our last post about communicating with people who do not share the exact same background. Anyway, since we couldn't run the technical test, we handed out stationery packets filled with art supplies and animal-topped pencils. We hadn’t done anything big, but the kids lit up instantly looking at different animals on their pencils. 

## Sometimes hardware is simpler and wonder is harder
Kids and play somehow go hand in hand, we realized that so many times on that field visit. Power grids and internet access are just the physical constraints we design around in resource constrained settings. But context, age, demographics are just as important.  We are almost sure that the best, technically correct, minimum solution may not work readily with projects involving kids. Somehow, we need to "keep our inner child alive", to remember bringing play into such projects. That wonder, and the memories they take out of a learning experience, is hopefully what will keep bringing them back to these classroom. Processing a mixed bag of emotions from everything we had seen, we brought back this important lesson to Zürich.
