Last week we had the chance to exhibit the [OLPC XO-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OLPC_XO) at the 
[Vintage Computer Festival in Solothurn](https://vintagecomputerfestival.ch)! It was a great 
experience, as we shared the history of this little laptop with so many other computer enthusiasts. 

# What is the OLPC XO-1?

As part of our mission, we want to make sure that we learn from earlier attempts at using technology
to drive equality: their successes, their failures and how they changed the world, hopefully for the
better. The [One Laptop Per Child](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Laptop_per_Child) initiative 
is a great example of such an attempt.

I first learned about OLPC around 2007 in the news. Back then I was a teenager, somewhat interested 
in computers, and this project felt charming and interesting. At a time when computers were bulky, 
expensive and normally rather fragile, the promise of a laptop for each child in the world at a price 
point of \$100 seemed too good to be true. In fact, as it turns out, it _was_ a bit too good to be 
true. The OLPC XO-1 never got down that price point, and instead went for around \$200.

OLPC was a non-profit organization that promised to bring digital education to developing countries.
Their vision included not only developing and distributing the computers, but also developing the 
software and educational tools to use them.

There are several goals of the project that strongly resonate with us:
- All code was open source, and the reason why was two-fold. On the one hand, this avoided paying for, 
at the time, costly software licenses like Windows. On the other hand, the computer was built by MIT
hackers, and in a way, for hackers. The intent was to make it possible for the kids to modify and 
adjust their own computer to their liking.
- The project had a noble educational goal. Like OLPC, we believe that technology can help in the 
classroom, though we might disagree on how to deploy this technology in the context of a classroom.

That said, not everything about the OLPC was rosy. It has been severely criticised by Morgan G. Ames 
in her book, [_The Charisma Machine_](https://morganya.org/charisma.html), a good read if you are 
interested in the topic. The computers were criticised for being difficult to use, an experience 
some of us have shared, and hard to repair. The project did not provide a way to introduce children 
to the world of computing, and assumed that the kids would "teach themselves". Such assumptions
made the design of the OLPC detached from the very people they were trying to help.

Twenty years later, the OLPC is mostly a memory of the past. Despite this, the computer had some innovative
ideas for its time that we wanted to showcase at VCFSO 2026, and it served as the perfect bridge between
retro computing and Tech for Equality.

# VCFSO 2026 - The experience

I find the Vintage Computer Festival to be a deeply inspiring event. You get to experience 
decades of technological progress in a single event, but it is more than this. It's about the people.
Everyone is friendly, encouraging and deeply passionate about the technology they show off. 

With the OLPC we wanted to share the same passion, while taking the opportunity to let people know who 
we are and what we do. We prepared several challenges for people to solve on the OLPC, from beginner 
level to nerd/hacker. 

Some challenges were about using the computer and experiencing the [Sugar Desktop Environment](https://wiki.sugarlabs.org/go/Getting_Started) 
and its collaborative features (like live editing a document on two OLPC computers at once). In contrast, 
our most advanced challenge required you to use [Open Firmware](https://www.openfirmware.info/Open_Firmware.html) 
and [Forth](https://forth-standard.org/) to build a boot menu that lets you boot from either the internal 
flash or the external SD card.

![A picture of our stand](/images/blog/vcfso2026/exhibit.jpeg)

We had a great experience talking to people from all sorts of backgrounds. Someone even brought a 
**pre-compiled version of Arch Linux 32 for the OLPC** that we booted during the festival. 
This was something we definitely did not anticipate! (Thanks, Andreas! We had a great time trying it 
out!)

We also found some time to experience the exhibits from our neighbours, and we even played with an
original IBM PC, scanning and then printing our logo!
